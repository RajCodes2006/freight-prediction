01Executive summary

From daily spot-checking the market to one graded recommendation

India's steel sector imports coking coal and other dry bulk from five continents, and pays for the ships that carry it almost entirely through daily, reactive spot-market decisions. Freight Prediction replaces that habit with a single decision engine that forecasts the market, checks the physical limits of the ports involved, prices the whole voyage, and returns one graded recommendation — with every number in it labelled by where it actually came from.

What it is

Freight Prediction is not a forecasting widget bolted onto a dashboard. Given a cargo scenario — commodity, quantity, origin, destination, contract length — it runs the full chain a chartering desk would run by hand: which vessel class actually fits the berths at both ends, what a complete voyage costs on that vessel, where the freight market is headed over the next 7 to 90 days, how reliable that forecast actually is, whether a multi-voyage contract beats staying on the spot market, and what the risk-adjusted numbers say to do about it. One request in, one graded recommendation out.

Why this matters right now

The system is built directly againstSmart India Hackathon Problem Statement 26006, issued by the Ministry of Steel through SAIL: a call for an intelligent freight-forecasting model that helps move India's bulk-cargo procurement away from repeated single spot-market fixtures and toward planned short- and medium-term multiple-voyage contracts — without losing sight of what India's East Coast ports can physically handle. That's a live, stated operational need inside a public-sector steel supply chain, not a hypothetical use case.

The core value proposition

  • Validated, multi-horizon forecasting — four vessel-class market indices, each forecast at 7/30/60/90 days, each carrying its own tested confidence grade rather than one point estimate presented as certain.
  • Physical reality, not just a market index — every recommendation is checked against real berth constraints, port congestion, and vessel economics before a number is ever quoted.
  • Radical transparency about data provenance — every figure in the response is tagged live, verified, historical, calculated, modelled, or assumed, so nobody mistakes a placeholder for a market quote.

02Problem statement

Chartering by reflex, in a market that doesn't reward reflexes

The problem statement's own framing is blunt: bulk-cargo procurement to India's East Coast runs on daily market exploration. That's not a data-availability problem so much as a data-integration problem — three genuinely different disciplines are being handled as separate manual steps instead of one connected decision.

What's actually happening today

Each cargo parcel is priced and fixed close to independently, off whatever the freight market happens to be doing that morning. There is no forward view carried from one fixture to the next, so there's no way to systematically prefer a good entry window over a mediocre one, or a short-term contract over a string of one-off spot fixtures, beyond a broker's memory and a spreadsheet.

Three problems stacked on top of each other

  • A forecasting problem. Freight markets for Handysize, Supramax, Panamax, and Capesize tonnage move on their own cycles, driven by global supply and demand that a single day's spot check can't reveal. Without a validated forward view, "is now a good time to fix a contract" is a guess dressed up as a decision.
  • A constraint problem. Cargo doesn't just need a ship — it needs a ship that physically fits the berths at both ends. Paradip, Visakhapatnam, Gangavaram, Gopalpur, Dhamra, the Sagar/Sandheads anchorage, and Haldia each cap out at different maximum LOA, beam, draft, and cargo-handling rate. A vessel that's cheap on paper but too large for the discharge berth doesn't save money; it just converts the saving into idle time and demurrage.
  • A financial-optimization problem. Whether a multi-voyage contract is actually worth fixing depends on the forecast, the contract's discount curve, and a genuine risk comparison against staying on the spot market — three numbers that need to be reconciled against each other before "fix" or "wait" means anything.

Why the reactive approach persists — and why it has to change

Nobody chooses the daily-spot-check approach because it's the best one; it persists because assembling the alternative by hand — a market read, a port-ops call, a cost spreadsheet, and a judgment call — doesn't scale to every parcel, every day. Published freight indices tell a desk where the market hasbeen; they don't tell it whether a specific vessel can even use a specific berth, or whether this month's contract math actually beats the risk of staying exposed to the spot market. Closing that gap is what makes a genuinely new approach necessary here, rather than an incrementally better spreadsheet.

Read the problem statement's own framingSIH 26006

The official description names the same three failure modes directly: an inability to identify optimal entry points for short- or mid-term charters amid volatile global freight markets; no systematic way to match vessel type to cargo size while respecting port infrastructure limits at both ends; and no forecasting mechanism to reduce vessel idle time or manage the risk of market volatility and port congestion. The expected solution it asks for — market-entry timing, vessel-type optimization against port constraints, idle-time management, and risk mitigation, delivered through a usable interface — maps directly onto the system described in this brief.

03The solution & UVP

One integrated decision engine, not three disconnected tools

Freight Prediction's answer to a three-part problem is a single pipeline that carries a cargo scenario through forecasting, physical constraints, and contract economics without ever losing the thread between them — and that says plainly, at every step, which numbers are live, which are validated model output, and which are still working assumptions.

What "integrated" concretely buys a chartering desk

  • Feasibility before price. Every candidate vessel is checked against real port and berth data first, so the system can never recommend a vessel that physically can't load or discharge the cargo at either port.
  • Graded forecasts, not one number. Each vessel-class market index is forecast at four horizons, and each of those forecasts carries its own validated HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW confidence — so a 7-day signal and a 90-day signal are never treated as equally trustworthy just because they came out of the same model family.
  • Full-voyage economics for every option, not just the winner. Freight, vessel time-charter, bunker, and port charges are priced for every feasible vessel, so the cheapest option is chosen by visible comparison rather than an opaque default.
  • Contract math that produces a number, not a hunch. A duration-based discount curve and a probability-weighted risk model turn "should we fix a contract" into an actual expected-savings figure.
  • A confidence-aware final call. The recommendation layer won't push toward fixing a contract just because the market is forecast to move, if that particular forecast hasn't validated as reliable. Direction and confidence are gated together.

The unique value proposition

Tools in this space tend to do one half of the job well: a market index that forecasts price and stops there, or a logistics/constraint tool with no market view behind it. Freight Prediction's differentiator is closing that loop end-to-end — and then being explicit about which part of the loop is real-time, which part is validated, and which part is still a labelled placeholder. That distinction is exposed to the user, not hidden behind a confident-looking number.

LIVEVERIFIEDHISTORICALCALCULATEDMODELLEDASSUMED

This six-state label is attached to every meaningful figure the API returns — freight rate, forecast level, congestion, sailing time, voyage cost, contract economics. Section 04 covers how it's built; Section 05 shows it attached to a real response.

Bridging 2012–2019 training data to today's market

The forecasting models are trained on historical Baltic dry-bulk data running from August 2012 to July 2019 — the current prototype dataset. Rather than quietly pretend the market froze there, the system includes a live-calibration layer: when a live benchmark feed is configured, it recalibrates thecurrent level of each vessel-class index to today's market, and lets the trained model supply the validatedshape of the forecast on top of that live level. Section 04 details the mechanism; it is one of the more deliberate pieces of engineering in the system.

04Technical architecture & innovation

Under the hood: stack, data, models, and the decision pipeline

This is the section judges will spend the most time in, so it's organized the way the system is actually built: the stack and why it was chosen, the data underneath it, the forecasting layer, the live-calibration engineering, the vessel/port/economics engine, and finally the eight-stage pipeline that ties all of it into one recommendation.

Stack and rationale

  • Backend — FastAPI (Python). The entire ML and data-science stack (pandas, scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, joblib) is native Python, so the API layer and the model layer share one runtime with nothing to keep in sync across a language boundary. FastAPI adds typed request validation via Pydantic and automatic interactive API documentation on top, at no extra integration cost.
  • Frontend — React 19 on Vite, deployed to Vercel, talking to the API over a typed Axios client with Vercel Analytics and Speed Insights wired in.
  • Deployment — Render hosts the FastAPI service; the repository is cleanly separated intobackend/ (API),models/ (forecasting + optimization engine), andfrontend/ (dashboard) — a structure that mirrors the actual pipeline rather than an arbitrary folder split.

Fig. 2 — Request flow: the dashboard calls one FastAPI endpoint, which orchestrates the forecasting models, the vessel/port engine, and (where configured) a live market feed, before every field in the response is stamped with its data provenance.

Data foundations

  • Historical training data. Baltic dry-bulk vessel-class indices — HSI (Handysize), SI (Supramax), PI (Panamax), CI (Capesize) — covering 1 August 2012 to 31 July 2019, cleaned and engineered into a multi-horizon feature set.
  • Verified port infrastructure. Berth-level records — max LOA, beam, draft, DWT, and cargo-handling rate — sourced from port authorities (for example, Paradip Port Authority) and markedVERIFIED, used directly in feasibility screening.
  • Verified port congestion. Median vessel-waiting-time data from Portcast, a maritime analytics provider, where a verified reading exists — and an explicitNO_VERIFIED_CURRENT_DATA flag, not a guess, everywhere it doesn't yet.
  • Live benchmark feed (optional). A server-side OilPriceAPI integration for current Baltic Dry Index and Baltic Capesize Index readings, cached 30 minutes, designed to fail soft.

The forecasting layer

Sixteen forecast targets — four vessel-class indices across four horizons — were each independently benchmarked. Three candidate algorithms (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest) were trained per target and compared against a naive persistence baseline, using achronological 80/20 train/test split (never randomly shuffled, which would leak future information backward into training) and then re-validated with a 40-pointwalk-forward evaluation over a held-out window from 15 March 2018 to 1 April 2019.

The winning method per target is recorded in a model registry — and the registry does not reflexively favor the more complex model. In7 of the 16 vessel-class/horizon combinations, walk-forward testing showed the boosted models did not reliably beat the simple persistence baseline, so the registry uses the baseline there instead. That's a validated choice the data forced, not a shortfall the team is glossing over.

Live-market calibration — the standout piece of engineering

The trained models operate in a 2012–2019 feature space, but chartering decisions get made today. Rather than retrain against data nobody has a continuous licensed feed for, the system recalibrates thecurrent level:

  • When a live BDI/BCI reading is available, a least-squares regression — fit once on the historical relationship between the composite Baltic indices and each vessel class — converts today's benchmark reading into today's calibrated HSI/SI/PI/CI level.
  • The trained model's predictedpercentage move is then applied on top of that live level, instead of on top of a stale 2019 number — recombining a validatedshape with a livelevel, rather than having to pick one or the other.
  • The resulting live-adjusted freight rate is clamped to a0.70×–1.50× band around the prototype baseline rate, so a bad reading from a third-party feed can't propagate into an unusable quote.
  • No API key configured, or the provider unreachable → the system continues on historical data without failing the request, and labels the responseHISTORICAL /PROTOTYPE_ASSUMPTION accordingly. This is exactly the path the Section 05 walkthrough took, and it still returned a complete, correctly-labelled answer.

The vessel & port engine

Four IMO-standard bulk-carrier classes are modelled with real DWT/LOA/beam/draft envelopes, from Handysize (10,000–49,999 DWT) through Capesize (100,000–250,000 DWT). Every candidate is checked against the actual berth records at both ports before a single dollar figure is produced — and a missing berth limit is treated as unknown, never guessed.

Sailing time: great-circle distance, corrected for the planet's landmasses

A straight great-circle line is the shortest path over asphere, not the shortest path overwater — for several real origins it cuts straight through a continent. The routing engine corrects for this with a small graph of real maritime chokepoints: the Suez Canal, Bab-el-Mandeb, the Bosphorus, the Cape of Good Hope, Cape Leeuwin, and the Singapore and Taiwan Straits.

Fig. 3 — Why the waypoint graph exists: the Australia-east-coast → India-east-coast lane is routed south of the continent via Cape Leeuwin, not across it. This is the actual routing applied to the Newcastle → Paradip leg used throughout Section 05.

Voyage economics, contracts, and risk

  • Total voyage cost = freight cost + vessel time-charter cost (transit + port days, priced at a day-rate by vessel class) + bunker + port charges — computed forevery feasible vessel, so the cheapest option is chosen by comparison, not assumption.
  • Contract pricing runs a duration-based discount curve, anchored at 1 / 3 / 6 / 12 months and linearly interpolated for anything in between, against the current spot rate.
  • Risk analysis prices a probability-weighted scenario range — 25% downside / 50% central / 25% upside, ±10% around the current rate — against the fixed contract cost, producing an actual expected-savings figure. This is the number the final recommendation is gated on, not just the direction of the forecast.

The master decision pipeline

Everything above feeds one eight-stage pipeline. Step through it below — each stage is the exact sequence the engine runs for every request.

01 Vessel feasibility

All four vessel classes are checked against the cargo quantity and the real berth records at both the origin and destination port. A class fails here with a plain reason —Insufficient cargo capacity, orbelow this vessel class's minimum realistic stem size — before any economics are even computed.

02 Full voyage economics

For every vessel that survives Stage 1, the engine prices the complete voyage: freight cost, vessel time-charter cost, bunker, and port charges. This runs for every feasible candidate, not only the one that ends up winning — so the comparison itself stays visible in the response.

03 Cheapest feasible vessel

Among the feasible, fully-priced candidates, the lowest total voyage cost is selected as the recommended vessel. When only one class is feasible — as in the Section 05 example — it wins by definition, but the losing classes and their reasons are still returned for transparency.

04 ML forecast

The selected vessel's underlying Baltic index (Supramax → SI, for example) is forecast at 7, 30, 60, and 90 days using whichever model the registry validated as best for each horizon.

05 Forecast confidence

Each of the four horizon forecasts is graded HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW from its own R², MAPE, and walk-forward improvement over the naive baseline — the rule set out in full above.

06 Contract economics

The current spot rate is priced against a multi-voyage contract using the duration-interpolated discount curve, producing a contract rate and a total contract cost for the planned number of voyages.

07 Risk analysis

A probability-weighted downside/central/upside scenario (25/50/25, ±10% around the current rate) is compared against the fixed contract cost to produce an expected-savings figure in dollars and in percent.

08 Final recommendation

Direction, confidence, and economics are combined into one of four actions —FIX_CONTRACT,CONSIDER_CONTRACT,MONITOR, orSPOT_OR_WAIT. A strong forecast alone is never enough to triggerFIX_CONTRACT if the confidence behind it is LOW — the engine downgrades to a more measured action instead.

The data-provenance layer

Every meaningful field in the API response — freight rate, forecast level, forecast model, sailing time, loading and discharge congestion, total voyage cost, contract economics — is accompanied by a status tag:LIVE, VERIFIED, HISTORICAL, CALCULATED, MODELLED, orASSUMED, plus a plain-language note explaining what that means for that specific field. It's an unusual thing to see built into a hackathon-stage prototype: most projects under time pressure either hard-code confident-looking numbers or blur the line between "this is real" and "this is a placeholder for now." This system chose to expose that line instead of hiding it.

06Technical challenges & triumphs

Where the engineering got hard, and what shipped instead of a shortcut

Challenge — the training data stops in 2019, the market doesn't

Triumph: rather than retrain against data nobody has a continuous licensed feed for, the live-calibration layer (Section 04) recalibrates only the currentlevel of each index from a live benchmark and lets the validated model supply theshape of the move — with a bounded multiplier so a bad reading can't produce a nonsensical quote, and a fail-soft path so a missing API key degrades the response instead of breaking it.

Challenge — a great circle cuts straight through continents

Triumph: the shortest path on a sphere isn't the shortest path over water. The chokepoint waypoint graph (Fig. 3) routes the Australia, US Gulf, Russian Black Sea, and Russian Pacific origins through the real straits and canals their traffic actually uses, each verified to stay over water rather than land on a convenient assumption.

Challenge — the pull to force machine learning everywhere

Triumph: walk-forward discipline, applied honestly. In 7 of 16 vessel-class/horizon combinations, a simple persistence baseline validated better than gradient boosting, and the model registry uses the baseline there — a harder call to make than shipping the "AI model" everywhere, and a more defensible one in front of the validation numbers.

Challenge — real port and congestion data is incomplete

Triumph: rather than fabricate a plausible-looking queue time for a port with no verified reading, the system labels itASSUMED orNO_VERIFIED_CURRENT_DATA and keeps going — a constraint that, worked through consistently, is what produced the full data-provenance layer described in Section 04.

Challenge — an optional third-party market feed that can be slow, wrong, or simply absent

Triumph: a 30-minute server-side cache, a try/except boundary around every external call, and a bounded output multiplier mean the live-market integration can only ever improve a response, never break one. The Section 05 walkthrough ran the fallback path end to end and still returned a complete, correctly-labelled answer.

07Feasibility, scalability & roadmap

Already running end-to-end. Built to extend, not rebuild.

Feasibility today

This isn't a notebook with a promising chart in it — it's a deployed, working system. The frontend is live on Vercel, the API is live on Render, the request/response contract is typed end to end, and the repository is cleanly modular:backend/,models/, andfrontend/ map directly onto the API layer, the forecasting/optimization engine, and the dashboard. Section 05's walkthrough is not a projection of what the system could do — it's what it does right now, on request.

Scalability

  • New geography is configuration, not architecture. Adding an origin port, a destination berth, or a new congestion source is a config and CSV addition against the existing port-loader and congestion modules, not a rewrite.
  • New forecasting methods slot into the existing registry pattern without touching the API layer — the pipeline already treats "which model serves this forecast" as a lookup, not a hard-coded branch.
  • The live-market adapter is provider-agnostic by design — swapping in a different benchmark source means implementing the same fetch/cache interface, not restructuring the calibration math around it.
  • FastAPI and Render scale horizontally behind the same typed contract the dashboard already speaks to.

A plausible path to deployment

The most direct fit is exactly where the problem statement points: a decision-support module for the Ministry of Steel's own import desks through SAIL. Beyond that, the same engine is a reasonable candidate for licensing — as a module or an API — to other public-sector bulk importers and to ship-broking desks who price the same origin-to-India trade lanes for private clients. None of this is secured; it's the shape of demand the problem statement itself describes.

Roadmap — the next six months

Every item below maps directly onto something the system already, honestly, labels as a prototype assumption today — this is a punch list against the system's own provenance layer, not a wish list.

Route-specific rate feeds

Replace the prototype freight-rate, bunker, and port-charge constants with licensed, route-specific market data.

Wider verified port coverage

Extend verified berth and congestion data beyond Paradip and Visakhapatnam to the remaining East Coast ports.

Real vessel-schedule routing

Replace the great-circle-plus-waypoint sailing-time estimate with AIS-based vessel-schedule data.

An extended, continuously updated training window

Push the historical dataset past July 2019 with a live feed, reducing how much weight the live-calibration layer has to carry.

Commodity- and route-specific modeling

Move from index-level forecasting to models that account for the specific commodity and trade lane, not just the vessel class.

Multi-user procurement workflows

Add roles and scenario history so a procurement team, not a single analyst, can work from the same evolving picture.

08Conclusion

A working answer to a real, stated problem

Freight Prediction takes a genuinely three-part problem — forecasting, physical port constraints, and contract economics — and closes it into one system that returns a single, graded recommendation instead of three disconnected data points a desk has to reconcile by hand.

What makes it credible rather than just ambitious is what Section 05 shows directly: run a real scenario, and the system doesn't just return an answer — it returns an answer it's honest about, downgrading its own confidence when the underlying forecast hasn't validated well rather than manufacturing certainty it hasn't earned. That's a harder standard to build to than a flashy demo, and it's the standard this system was held to throughout: real port data over guesses, validated models over the flashiest algorithm, and a labelled assumption over a hidden one.

It was built against one SAIL problem statement. If the roadmap in Section 07 lands, the architecture underneath it — modular, provider-agnostic, config-driven — is already shaped to become something bigger than the submission that started it: a standard decision layer for how India's steel sector, and potentially its wider bulk-import economy, buys shipping capacity.
